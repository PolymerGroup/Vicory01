Peptides sit in an unusual spot in the laboratory supply chain: small enough to be made by solid-phase synthesis, complex enough that two suppliers can quote the "same" product and deliver materially different material. This primer covers what the product actually is, how it is made, and which numbers on the paperwork deserve your attention.

## What a research peptide is

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids linked by amide bonds. In the research supply market the term usually covers chains of roughly 2–60 residues — from tripeptides such as KPV to modified analogues of endogenous hormones. Beyond that length the economics and analytical profile shift toward recombinant proteins and fermentation-derived material, which is a different supply chain entirely.

Most catalogue peptides fall into three groups:

- **Linear synthetic peptides** — the simplest case: a defined sequence, a defined counter-ion, one main HPLC peak.
- **Modified peptides** — containing D-amino acids, N-terminal acetylation, C-terminal amidation, lipidation, PEGylation or disulfide bridges. Modifications change solubility, stability and the analytical method required.
- **Blends and ancillary reagents** — fixed-composition mixtures, bacteriostatic water, dilute acetic acid. These are not peptides at all but are routinely bought alongside them.

## How they are made

Commercial quantities are produced by **solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS)**: the chain is built on a resin, cleaved, then purified by preparative reverse-phase HPLC. The purified pool is exchanged into the target salt (usually acetate or TFA), filtered, filled into vials and lyophilised.

Two practical consequences follow:

1. **Purity is a distribution, not a switch.** Every lot contains trace deletion sequences, truncated fragments and oxidation products. What changes between suppliers is how aggressively the crude is purified and how honestly the result is reported.
2. **The counter-ion is part of the product.** Acetate and TFA salts differ in mass, hygroscopicity and pH behaviour after reconstitution. A certificate that omits the salt form is describing an incomplete product.

## Why 10-vial kits

A kit is a manufacturing and documentation unit. Ten vials come from one fill session, share one lot number and one certificate, and are packed together with desiccant. For a laboratory that is convenient: a single COA covers the whole study, and there is no ambiguity about which lot a result came from.

For buyers comparing quotes, it also removes a common source of confusion. Prices are quoted **per kit of 10 vials** in this catalogue, not per vial, so the per-vial cost is simply the kit price divided by ten.

## The numbers that matter

| Figure | What it tells you | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purity (area %) | Share of the UV signal that is the target peak | RP-HPLC at 214 nm or 280 nm |
| Identity | Whether the mass matches the theoretical sequence | ESI-MS, deconvoluted |
| Peptide content | How much peptide mass the vial actually holds | Mass balance / nitrogen assay |
| Water content | Residual moisture after lyophilisation | Karl Fischer titration |
| Endotoxin | Bacterial endotoxin load, where applicable | LAL assay |
| Salt form | Acetate, TFA, or other counter-ion | Stated on the COA |

A certificate that reports only "≥99%" and nothing else is not a certificate — it is a marketing line.

## How to evaluate a supplier

Three questions separate serious suppliers from intermediaries:

1. **Can you send the COA for the lot you will ship, before I order?** A real supplier has the document already; an intermediary has to ask someone else.
2. **What is the lot numbering scheme, and does it appear on the vial label?** Traceability only works if the label and the certificate agree.
3. **Which analytical method produced the purity figure, and at what wavelength?** Area normalisation at 214 nm and at 280 nm are not interchangeable.
