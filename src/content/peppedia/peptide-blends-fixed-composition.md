A blend is not a bigger peptide. It is a formulation: two or more peptides (sometimes plus an ancillary) filled into one vial at a defined ratio. That changes what you must specify, and it changes what a certificate can prove.

## The specification you should demand

For every blend SKU, the documentation should state:

| Item | Example | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Components | BPC-157, GHK-Cu, TB-500 | Identity of each part |
| Per-component mass | 10 mg / 50 mg / 10 mg | The ratio is the product |
| Total mass per vial | 70 mg | Consistency with the label |
| Vial count per kit | 10 | Pack format |
| Analytical method per component | RP-HPLC, separate injections | A co-eluting method proves nothing |
| Homogeneity | Fill validation or blend uniformity | Powder blends can segregate |

"The blend contains BPC-157, GHK-Cu and TB-500" is a description. The table above is a specification.

## Why a single purity figure is misleading

If three peptides elute in the same window, a single area-percentage purity cannot tell you:

- whether all three are present;
- whether the ratio matches the label;
- whether one component is degraded while another is intact.

The defensible approach is a method that resolves each component (or separate injections per component), with a reported assay for each and an acceptance range for the ratio. For a blend, **ratio accuracy is the quality attribute**, not overall peak purity.

## Compounding realities

Blends are made by weighing and mixing lyophilised powders, then filling vials. Three failure modes follow:

1. **Segregation** — powders of different particle size and density separate during handling. Good suppliers validate uniformity after mixing.
2. **Fill drift** — small target masses mean fill accuracy dominates the final ratio.
3. **Moisture uptake** — every handling step exposes hygroscopic powders to air; blends typically carry higher water content than single peptides.

None of these are visible in a purity number. They show up as inconsistent reconstitution or as component ratios that drift from vial to vial.

## Sourcing checklist for blends

- Ask for the **per-component masses** in writing, on the specification and on the label.
- Ask which **method** was used for each component, and whether it resolves the others.
- Ask for the **water content** of the finished blend.
- For repeat orders, ask whether the ratio is fixed by a written formula or re-derived each time.
- If the blend is private-label, ask for the **fill validation** approach.

## When a blend is the wrong choice

If your study needs to vary the amount of one component independently, a blend removes that ability. Blends are convenient for procurement and inconvenient for experimental design. Buy them when the fixed ratio is what you actually want to study.
